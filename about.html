I'm not an agency. That's the point.

You will talk to the person who writes the code. There is no account manager translating what you said into a ticket, and nothing gets quietly passed to a junior. It means I take fewer projects than an agency would, and it means the person who understands your system in month six is the same one who built it in month one.

I'm a Python engineer based in Istanbul. Most of my work is backend: async FastAPI services, Telegram bots, data pipelines, the automation layer that sits between a business and the platforms it depends on. I've got more than fourteen systems in production and I maintain them, which is a different discipline from launching them.

Why I build differently from most freelancers

Because I run my own products as well as client work. I operate a crypto data platform, a student admissions bot, and a Persian-language channel with around eighteen thousand subscribers. When something I built breaks at two in the morning, it's my phone that goes off.

That changes how you write software. You stop shipping things that are clever and start shipping things that recover on their own. You log properly the first time. You assume the third-party API will fail, because you've been on the wrong side of it. Clients get the benefit of a system built by someone who has to live with systems.

It also means I'll tell you when a project isn't worth doing. I have my own work to go back to, so I'm not trying to fill a bench.

Working in three languages

Persian is my first language, I work in English, and I speak Turkish. That matters more than it sounds. If your customers write to you in Persian or Turkish, I can read what they actually said, test that the bot's replies sound natural rather than machine-translated, and lay out a right-to-left interface that a native reader doesn't find subtly wrong.

Most developers outsource that and hope. It's the single most common reason multilingual projects come out looking amateur.
